## Expanded draft before page compression

### Purpose and problem

Fix the Web works towards digital experiences designed around people's intentions, interests and relationships. We build alternatives, investigate what helps, and advocate for the choices and access that make better experiences possible. Villow is our first substantial project, not the boundary of the mission.

We should be able to enjoy what digital services offer without accepting every design decision that comes with them. People learn, laugh, discover interests and find one another online. Giving these things up is a poor substitute for improving the services that provide them. The usefulness is precisely why this work matters.

Time spent is not the same as time well spent. Continued watching may reflect enjoyment; it may also reflect habit, an endless next item or a lack of a natural decision point. A person's own judgment matters. A funny video, a long evening with friends or a restful distraction can be worthwhile. Fix the Web should not define a good life in minutes saved or productivity gained.

A following-feed option does not by itself create meaningful choice. The alternative needs enough care to be attractive. Controls need understandable information, accessible placement and ways to revisit earlier choices. Platforms should give finishing as much design attention as starting. This is a practical design agenda, not a demand that every service copy Villow.

### Villow as the first example

Villow demonstrates a different way to use YouTube. A finite feed makes an ending visible. Adjustable boundaries let people choose how watching fits their day. An earlier decision can retain some weight through a no-extension streak without making an override impossible. Short videos and long sessions remain compatible with deliberate choices. The point is to support the person's intentions, including the choice to revise them.

Reflection is part of a loop: decide, observe, consider and adjust. Optional worthwhile ratings belong alongside timing, browsing and watching patterns. The app's explained suggestions connect those patterns to concrete controls. For example, a pattern of watching a creator's 5–30 minute videos and skipping other lengths can inform a suggested duration filter. The person accepts or dismisses the suggestion. The illustration is not a real user's data or a measured effect, and these transparent rules are not generative AI.

An attractive subscriptions experience needs channel-specific controls, useful discovery and continuity across supported usage. People who want YouTube's recommendations can use the extension on YouTube. Combining it with the app lets them choose videos there and save them into Villow, with supported usage and limits connected. This does not reproduce YouTube's personalised recommendations inside the app or monitor every device.

Social value is more than accountability. Passing along a recommendation with a personal note and watching together are positive uses in their own right. Sharing usage or goals is a separate opt-in choice. Evaluation must ask whether accountability is useful, pressuring or intrusive, and whether features become chores. Hosts' database access is distinct from what members deliberately share with friends.

Villow is a working alternative and a starting point for learning. Its features make design choices concrete. Their effects need evaluation. The case page should separate those two statements once, then give real questions: stopping while retaining valued viewing; commitments without pressure; reflection without interruption; discoverability without endless administration; connection without surveillance. Participation and retention do not alone establish benefit. Useful evidence includes negative experiences, reasons for leaving, usability failures and who is excluded by setup requirements. Private viewing data is not automatically research data.

### The changes sought

First, make meaningful choice usable. Recommendation choices, discovery alternatives, stopping points and boundaries should be understandable and cared for. Second, make responsible experimentation possible. Independent builders and researchers need clear permissions for a defined question, with appropriate protections and a route to resolve uncertainty. Third, allow useful alternatives to continue when people want to use them, through workable arrangements for ongoing operation. Creators, privacy, security, costs and platform responsibilities belong in those arrangements.

Data access to understand a platform, permission to run an interface experiment and permission to operate an ongoing public product are distinct. A research study's end should not make the question of continuing use invisible, but neither does research authorisation grant an unrestricted right to run a service. Platform-native adoption is another essential route: an effective idea can reach many people without requiring them to set up a separate service.

A concrete access investigation starts with a finite-subscriptions experiment. Identify required content metadata, playback, permissions, user consent and measurements. Record separately what documented APIs make possible, what quotas or hosting make difficult, what published rules constrain, and what feature-specific interpretation remains unverified. Educational intent or independent value alone cannot establish compliance. This record should support a scoped proposal, not a prediction of shutdown.

### A wider set of possibilities

The next ideas are proposals, not funded programmes or features on a delivery schedule. A cross-ecosystem wellbeing tool could let a person understand use across the devices they actually own. It must account for overlapping activity and background media; raw totals can mislead. Local processing, limited summaries, optional synchronisation and separately chosen sharing should be evaluated from the outset. Existing ecosystem-level tools are a starting point, not something this proposal invents.

On YouTube, a correction could reach someone after they have already watched a video. Current Notes provide a precedent; the proposed extension is broader participation and notification to viewers from the prior two weeks. This raises a watch-history, consent, accuracy and notification-burden problem, not just an interface problem.

Creators could offer meaningful next actions that viewers can save. Limited recommendations could give viewers' considered judgment a different role in discovery, beyond Hype's defined scope. Optional AI could assist with creator-reviewed disclosed chapters, source-grounded questions about a video, or the research and drafting work behind notes. None makes the AI a truth authority. Authentic conversation also needs protection from manipulation without casually labelling people bots or forcing invasive identity checks.

The principles may carry into other platforms, games, messaging and AI. Designs do not transfer unchanged. The proposals page provides room to consider concrete ideas without pretending one founder is already running projects in each area.

### Founder and participation

Keep the original personal message accessible, with Nicholas named as founder of Villow and Fix the Web. Its central motivation is concern for mental health, attention, relationships and trust in democracy, paired with recognition of social media's value. The work exists to explore and improve possibilities, not merely seek validation for an app.

Invite specific contributions through the existing email route: design an evaluation of one feature; examine whether a control is understandable and accessible; investigate a documented access question; help develop the privacy requirements of one proposal; introduce the project to a relevant researcher, creator or policy contact. No anonymous feedback is solicited as implicit consent to collect viewing data. No fake issue board, study signup, donor flow or download is added.
