Submission on the Online Safety Amendment (Digital Duty 
of Care) Bill 2026
Nicholas Nucifora
Developer of Villow
villow.app
User empowerment should extend beyond choosing a feed
I welcome the exposure draft's focus on how online services are designed, rather than looking only at 
the harmful content that may appear on them.
I am an Australian software developer currently building Villow, an alternative interface for using 
YouTube that I designed around a different question from most social-media products:
How can an online service help people use it more intentionally, including helping them stop 
using it when they would rather be doing something else?
I began this project independently, before the release of the current exposure draft. Building it has 
forced me to think in practical detail about recommender systems, endless feeds, stopping points, 
time limits, user choice, reflection, accountability, and the tension between a user's longer-term 
intentions and their immediate impulses.
I am not presenting Villow's individual features as proven interventions. It is a working prototype that 
now needs meaningful user testing. Rather, I want to share some of the design problems I 
encountered, because I think they are relevant to the eventual design of the Bill's user empowerment 
tools.
I support giving people a genuine choice between personalised recommendations and content from 
people they deliberately follow. My main suggestion is that meaningful user empowerment should 
not end at that choice.
Engagement is not the same thing as value
A fundamental problem with engagement-based recommendation is that the signals a platform can 
easily optimise — clicks, watch time, retention and continued use — are not necessarily the outcomes 
the user themselves values.
There is clearly some correlation: people often continue watching because they enjoy something. But 
engagement can also continue because a person is acting habitually, has been drawn into an endless 
sequence of content, or simply has not had a natural opportunity to stop and reflect. They may spend 
considerable time on something without afterwards feeling it was how they wanted to spend that time.
This mismatch can also create broader risks. Systems tuned primarily for attention may reward 
clickbait, sensationalism, misinformation, outrage or polarising material when those patterns 
successfully attract engagement.
The question I became interested in while designing Villow was therefore not simply:
How do we remove recommendation algorithms?
It was:
How do we design an online experience in which people remain conscious participants in what 
they are doing?
A useful online service should make it easy to continue when a person deliberately wants to continue, 
but it should also make disengagement natural.
Design for stopping, not only continuing
A chronological or following-only feed can still autoplay content, endlessly present more material, and 
give users few natural moments to decide whether they actually want to continue.
These are separate design choices from the recommendation algorithm itself.
For example, Villow experiments with a finite subscriptions feed rather than loading content 
indefinitely. The aim is that a user can eventually reach the end rather than being presented with an 
effectively unlimited supply of material.
Short-form video illustrates this distinction: deliberately choosing a short because it interests you is 
very different from repeatedly swiping to reveal unknown content. The format does not necessarily 
need to disappear; the way users move through it can be redesigned.
Villow similarly removes autoplay in most situations. Starting another piece of content should 
generally require another decision from the user — even a small moment to consider, “Do I actually 
want another video?”
Villow also avoids placing recommendations beside the video itself. Discovery can happen elsewhere, 
but once somebody chooses a video, I want the interface to help them remain present with that 
choice. If they no longer want to watch it, stopping should itself become a conscious decision rather 
than immediately presenting another algorithmic path forward.
The principle is broader than any of these individual decisions:
Platforms should design as deliberately for the moment a person stops as they currently design 
for the moment a person continues.
Reflection can itself be a product feature
Giving somebody controls is useful, but a person first needs enough information to decide whether 
they want to change those controls.
One feature I have experimented with asks the user, after viewing something, a very simple question:
Was this worth your time?
This is deliberately different from asking whether somebody “liked” a video.
A person might enjoy something while still deciding afterwards that it was not how they wanted to 
spend that time. Conversely, entertainment can absolutely be worthwhile even when it serves no 
productive purpose.
The purpose is not to decide what content is good for the user. It is to give the user a quick opportunity 
to make that judgement for themselves.
The interaction is designed to involve as little effort as possible. On mobile, for example, giving that 
feedback can be reduced to a simple swipe. The aim is to make reflection feel like a natural and 
satisfying part of using the product rather than another digital-wellbeing chore.
That small interaction creates a moment in which the user considers what they just watched. It can 
also produce useful information over time. Someone can see not merely how many hours they spent 
watching videos, but what proportion of that viewing they themselves subsequently considered 
worthwhile.
Villow similarly exposes information about time spent watching, time spent browsing, when usage 
occurred and patterns in how the service is opened.
The broader principle is that user empowerment requires information as well as controls.
However, there is another difficult balance here. Excessive warnings or wellbeing prompts can 
become irritating, judgemental or guilt-inducing and eventually be ignored.
The challenge is not simply showing users more information. It is to make reflection quick, intuitive, 
useful and even enjoyable, without overwhelming them.
Make the alternative to engagement algorithms better
Another lesson from Villow is that giving someone the ability to turn off an algorithm does not 
necessarily make the alternative attractive.
If the personalised feed is highly refined while the following feed is overwhelming or poorly organised, 
the user technically has a choice, but one option remains substantially easier to use.
Villow therefore puts significant effort into making subscriptions themselves more manageable.
Users can exercise much more granular control over the creators they already chose to follow. Rather 
than treating every subscription identically, people can shape how much content they want from 
different sources and what types of content they want to see.
The system can also identify patterns in the user's own behaviour and make suggestions to make 
these more granular controls more intuitive. For example, it may notice that a particular source is 
consuming a disproportionate amount of time or that the user consistently values one type of content 
from a creator but not another.
Importantly, these suggestions do not silently modify the person's experience; the system explains the 
pattern, and proposes an action. It’s up to the user to decide whether the suggestion makes sense.
Turn intentions into commitments without removing control
Time limits raise a deeper question than simply whether a user can set one:
What should happen when a person's earlier intention conflicts with their immediate impulse?
My approach with Villow is not to make limits impossible to override. The user remains in control. But I 
also do not think a self-imposed limit is very meaningful if it can be dismissed without consequence 
the moment it becomes inconvenient.
Simply requiring somebody to open settings and increase a limit already exists in operating-system 
wellbeing tools. The more interesting question is how a product can give a person's earlier decision 
some continuing weight.
One experiment in Villow is a “no-extension” streak.
A user can increase their limit whenever they choose, but doing so resets a streak based on keeping to 
the limits they previously set for themselves.
The commitment is therefore not absolute. The person can still decide that today is different, or that 
the original limit no longer makes sense. But overriding it becomes a conscious choice with a visible 
consequence rather than an automatic dismissal.
Optional social features can extend the same principle further. Users may choose to share goals or 
limits with friends, creating accountability that they have deliberately opted into.
This repurposes a pattern commonly seen across digital products. Instead of streaks, competition and 
social reinforcement rewarding continued engagement, the same mechanisms can reward adherence 
to goals the users chose themselves.
The important principle is not friction for its own sake. A few extra clicks can easily become 
meaningless.
The more interesting design problem is how technology can help a person follow through on an 
intention they themselves chose, while still preserving their ability to change their mind.
Too little resistance makes commitments easy to abandon. Too much makes the product paternalistic 
or frustrating. Finding the right balance requires experimentation rather than assuming that simply 
offering a limit is enough.
An opportunity for experimentation
One of the strongest lessons I have taken from building Villow is how little settled design knowledge 
there is in this area.
There has been enormous experimentation in making digital products easier to enter, more engaging 
and harder to leave. Designing a useful product that also succeeds when a person decides they have 
had enough is a much less developed problem.
Once the objective changes from “maximise engagement” to “help the user use the service in the 
way they genuinely want”, many product decisions that currently appear inevitable suddenly 
become open questions.
Questions around reflection, accountability, recommendation, stopping and the balance between 
helpful intervention and annoyance remain largely open, and the answers will differ slightly between 
services.
I do not think these questions can be answered well through a single set of assumptions made at the 
beginning of the regulatory process.
They need prototypes, user testing, iteration and independent evaluation. That experimentation 
also requires access. Villow is possible in part because YouTube is one of the only social media 
platforms to provides access to APIs and an embeddable player. Independent researchers and 
prototype developers would find it much harder to prototype alternative designs for other digital 
services. Appropriately scoped, privacy-preserving research access could help make this 
experimentation possible.
Different platforms will also require different solutions. Designing intentionally around YouTube has 
made clear to me how specific many of these problems are to the structure of the service itself. 
Different platforms will require different solutions; what works for long-form video may not work for 
short-form, messaging or other digital environments.
Rather than expecting platforms — or government — to already know the ideal implementation, I 
would encourage an environment in which alternative designs can be built, tested and compared, with 
successful approaches informing future standards and user empowerment tools.
There may also be value in supporting independent experimentation in this area. Platforms have 
understandable commercial incentives when designing their own engagement systems. Independent 
projects can explore different objectives, test approaches that may run against conventional 
engagement metrics, and contribute evidence about what users actually find useful.
Independent experimentation does not need to be large or slow. These projects can quickly prototype 
alternative ways of using existing platforms and test ideas that the platforms themselves may have 
little commercial incentive to explore. Their purpose is not to replace those platforms, but to 
demonstrate and evaluate approaches that could inform future requirements, then given clear 
timeframes for platforms to implement them. Villow is one example of what this kind of independent 
experimentation can produce.
I would be very interested in contributing to that work: demonstrating Villow, sharing the reasoning 
behind its design decisions, participating in user research, or helping test different approaches to user 
empowerment.
The central principle I would encourage the Government to preserve is simple:
Giving people control over what appears in their feed is an important step. Meaningful digital 
agency also requires helping people understand their behaviour, act deliberately on their own 
intentions, and disengage when they choose to.
